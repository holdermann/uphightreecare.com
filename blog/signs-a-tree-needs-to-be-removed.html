7 Signs a Tree Needs to Come Down (and 3 That Just Look Scary)

October 11, 2026 · Up High Tree Care

Nobody wants to take down a healthy tree — least of all us. But along the Wasatch Front, where wet spring snows and canyon winds load-test every canopy twice a year, a genuinely failing tree is a roof problem waiting for a date. Here’s how we tell the difference on estimates across the Salt Lake Valley.

7 signs that usually mean removal

  1. Mushrooms or conks at the base. Fungal fruiting bodies on the lower trunk or root flare usually mean rot where the tree needs strength most. By the time you see them, the decay inside is well established.
  2. A new or increasing lean. Trees can live happily with an old lean. A new lean — especially with lifted or cracked soil on the back side — means roots are failing. That’s an urgent call, not a watch-and-wait.
  3. Hollow or badly decayed trunk. Trees can stand with some hollow, but when a screwdriver sinks into punky wood around much of the circumference, the safety margin is gone.
  4. A dead top. Dieback that starts at the crown and works down typically means root or vascular failure. The tree is telling you how this ends.
  5. Major included-bark splits. When two leaders squeeze bark between them instead of fusing, the union is a crack waiting for a snow load. Cabling saves some; many are past it.
  6. More than half the canopy dead or storm-wrecked. A tree can shed a limb and recover. A tree that lost most of its crown to one storm rarely rebuilds into anything safe.
  7. Root damage from construction. Trenching, grading, or a new driveway through the root zone often kills a mature tree on a two-to-five-year delay. If the decline has started, it doesn’t reverse.

3 things that look scary but usually aren’t

  • Bleeding sap in spring — maples and birches do this after winter pruning or frost cracks. Messy, mostly harmless.
  • A big dead limb in an otherwise green canopy — that’s a trimming job, not a removal.
  • Surface roots heaving the lawn — normal for cottonwoods and silver maples in the valley’s irrigated yards. Annoying, not dangerous.

What an honest assessment looks like

When we look at a questionable tree in Holladay, Taylorsville, or anywhere in the valley, you get one of three answers: it’s fine, maintain it — it’s fixable, here’s the prune or thin that fixes it — or it needs to come out, here’s the firm price. We don’t sell removals that trees don’t need; being family-owned means our name rides on every call we make.
