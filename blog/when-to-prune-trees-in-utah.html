When to Prune Trees in Utah: A Season-by-Season Guide

October 19, 2026 · Up High Tree Care

Ask ten neighbors when to prune a tree in Utah and you’ll get ten answers. Here’s the calendar our crew actually works by across the Salt Lake Valley — and the handful of species that break the rules.

Late winter (February–March): the main event

For most shade trees — honeylocust, ash, oak, linden, sycamore — dormant-season pruning is ideal. The leaves are off, so the tree’s whole architecture is visible: crossing limbs, weak unions, deadwood, all of it easy to see and reach. Cuts made in late winter sit for only a few weeks before spring growth starts sealing them, and insects and fungal spores are at their annual low.

This is when we do most structural pruning — the health-and-strength work that’s different from clearance trimming.

The rule-breakers

  • Maples and birches bleed sap heavily from late-winter cuts. It looks alarming (it mostly isn’t harmful), but we prefer to prune them in midsummer, after the leaves have fully hardened off.
  • Elms should not be pruned in spring and early summer. Fresh elm cuts attract the bark beetles that spread Dutch elm disease, and the Salt Lake Valley still has a lot of elm canopy worth protecting. Elm work belongs in the dormant season.
  • Fruit trees (apple, pear, peach, cherry, apricot) get their structural prune in late winter before bud break, with light summer touch-ups to control vigorous growth and keep fruit within picking height.
  • Spring bloomers like crabapple, lilac, and flowering pear are best pruned right after they finish flowering — prune them in winter and you’re cutting off this year’s show.

Summer: clearance and corrections

Clearance work — limbs off the roof, over the driveway, away from the service drop — can happen any time of year, and summer is when the problems are most visible. Summer is also the season to spot and remove deadwood: dead limbs stand out against a leafed canopy.

Fall: the one season we mostly avoid

Pruning cuts made in fall seal slowly, right as fungal spores peak. Unless it’s deadwood or a hazard, we’d rather wait a few months for the dormant window. What fall is good for: crown thinning and weight reduction before the heavy snow season — if your trees took damage last winter, October is the time to get ahead of this one.

The exception that overrides everything

Hazards don’t wait for the calendar. A cracked limb over the roof, a split leader, storm damage hanging in the canopy — that gets dealt with now, whatever the month. If you’ve got one of those in Salt Lake City or anywhere in the valley, call us at (801) 890-7432 and we’ll move fast.
